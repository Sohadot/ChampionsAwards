Methodology · DDI v1.0

The Deservingness Index

A single number is never the point. The point is that the number is produced the same way every time, from inputs anyone can inspect, argue with, and reproduce.

What the index measures

The Deservingness Index (DDI) estimates the assessed structural significance of a contribution — how much a person or work actually changed, independent of whether an institution chose to reward it. It is a research instrument, not a verdict. It is deliberately separate from recognition itself, so that the two can be compared.

The six dimensions

Each dimension is scored from 0 to 100 against the rubric below, then combined using fixed weights that sum to 1.00. The weights encode a claim we are willing to defend: that durable, independently verified, hard-to-substitute impact matters most.

DimensionWeightWhat a high score requires
Structural impact0.28The contribution changed a field, system, or population at a deep level — not incrementally.
Independent verification0.20The impact is confirmed by primary or independent sources, not by the actor's own account or a single institution.
Counterfactual uniqueness0.16The contribution was unlikely to have arrived as soon, or in the same form, from someone else.
Temporal durability0.16The significance held or grew over time rather than fading with its moment.
Breadth of benefit0.12The benefit reached widely, not only a narrow or already-privileged group.
Transparency of attribution0.08Who did what is clear and traceable, reducing the risk of misattributed credit.

The formula

The composite score is a straightforward weighted sum:

DDI = 0.28·impact + 0.20·verification + 0.16·uniqueness + 0.16·durability + 0.12·breadth + 0.08·attribution

Because the weights sum to 1.00 and every input is on a 0–100 scale, the result is always between 0 and 100. No hidden terms, no post-hoc adjustments.

Reading the result

BandScoreInterpretation
Landmark85–100Field- or era-defining significance under this assessment.
Major70–84Substantial, durable, broadly verified contribution.
Substantial55–69Clear significance with meaningful limits or open questions.
Moderate40–54Real but bounded, contested, or not yet durable.
Limited0–39Significance is narrow or not yet established under this model.

The recognition gap

Deservingness is only half the story. Alongside the DDI we record an observed recognition level (0–100) — how much formal recognition the subject actually received. The recognition gap is the difference:

gap = DDI − observed recognition

A large positive gap is the signature of the Unawarded Archive: assessed significance that the recognition architecture failed to match. A large negative gap flags recognition that may exceed the durable, verifiable record — the question the awards and recognition-system clusters exist to ask.

A second instrument: Recognition Legitimacy (RLS v1.0)

Individuals are scored with the DDI. Systems — prizes, honors, ranking bodies — are assessed separately with the Recognition Legitimacy Score. It does not judge whether any single laureate deserved their award; it judges whether the process that produced the awards is sound. Five weighted dimensions, summing to 1.00:

DimensionWeightWhat a high score requires
Process integrity0.25Nomination and selection follow rigorous, consistent, documented procedure.
Representational breadth0.20The eligible pool and actual recipients span geographies, groups, and sub-fields.
Historical track record0.20Past selections have held up as sound and durable rather than eroding over time.
Transparency0.18Criteria, and ideally deliberations and records, are publicly available.
Independence0.17The body is insulated from political and commercial capture.

RLS = 0.25·process + 0.20·breadth + 0.20·track_record + 0.18·transparency + 0.17·independence

BandScoreInterpretation
Exemplary85–100A process others can reasonably treat as a benchmark.
Strong70–84Sound process with limited, identifiable weaknesses.
Adequate55–69Legitimate but with real structural gaps.
Contested40–54Serious, recurring questions about the process.
Fragile0–39Legitimacy rests more on prestige than on defensible process.

RLS is applied in the recognition-systems cluster and available in the calculator alongside the DDI.

Limits we accept

  • Scores are estimates from human judgment against a rubric, not measurements. Two careful evaluators can disagree, and the model surfaces that rather than hiding it.
  • The weights are a defensible choice, not a law of nature. Changing them changes results; that is why the version is stamped on every page.
  • The model can encode the same biases it studies. The editorial protocol and public corrections are the countermeasure, not a claim of neutrality.

Versioning

This is DDI v1.0. Any change to dimensions, weights, or bands increments the version, and prior versions remain on record so past scores stay interpretable. Reproducibility is the whole point.
